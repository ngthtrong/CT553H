# Imbalance handling, few-shot PLM classification, and generative augmentation of protein sequences (2021–2026)

## Q1. What evidence (2022–2026) shows generative augmentation helps or does not help downstream protein classifiers? Any controlled studies vs simple baselines (oversampling, class weighting, focal loss, embedding-space SMOTE, mixup)?

### Takeaway

There is **no published, properly controlled head-to-head study** comparing PLM-generative sequence augmentation against the cheap standard imbalance baselines (class weighting, focal loss, embedding-space SMOTE, mixup) on protein *family* classification. What exists is (i) one rigorous study of *string-manipulation* augmentation on TAPE showing task-dependent and partly inexplicable gains, (ii) a scattering of application papers (including the advisors' own mCNN-GenEfflux) that report very large gains from generative augmentation but with no baseline-matched ablation and no test-set-identity audit, and (iii) tabular/non-protein evidence that deep generative oversampling only modestly beats SMOTE. This is the single clearest open gap in the area.

### Cited Findings

**The one careful protein-sequence augmentation study (and it is not generative):**

- Shen et al., "Improving Generalizability of Protein Sequence Models with Data Augmentations" (2021) tested six string manipulations — dictionary/alanine replacement, global random shuffling, local random shuffling (50-residue windows), sequence reversion, subsampling (50-residue regions), and pairwise combinations — on four TAPE tasks. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2021.02.18.431877](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.18.431877v1.full)
- Best linear-evaluation gains in that paper: remote homology **+1.9% to +9.3% accuracy**, secondary structure **+0.8% to +4.8%**, stability **+0.064 Spearman ρ**, fluorescence **+0.105 ρ**. Under full fine-tuning, gains persisted for stability, remote homology and secondary structure, but fluorescence fell **within ~1σ of baseline**. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2021.02.18.431877](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.18.431877v1.full)
- Two conclusions from that paper matter methodologically: "different downstream tasks benefit from different protein augmentations", and **even information-destroying augmentations (sequence shuffling) sometimes improved performance**, which the authors attribute to the model learning permutation-invariant features. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2021.02.18.431877](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.18.431877v1.full)

**The advisors' own paper (mCNN-GenEfflux) — large reported gains, weak ablation:**

- mCNN-GenEfflux fine-tunes `nferruz/ProtGPT2` **separately per efflux family**, after removing redundancy at a **30% pairwise identity threshold using PSI-BLAST**, then feeds PSSM features to a multi-window CNN + FCNN. Dataset built from five efflux superfamilies via TCDB. [verified from local PDF text] — /home/ngthtrong/CT553H/docs/common/advisor/Prof Ho/1-s2.0-S1476927125002567-main.pdf ; [doi:10.1016/j.compbiolchem.2025.108595](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compbiolchem.2025.108595)
- Reported effect of adding ProtGPT2-generated sequences: **Class B MCC 0.4397 → 0.9327**; **Class C accuracy 0.8977 → 0.9668 and MCC 0.7668 → 0.9331**. The abstract frames this as evidence that "sequences generated by ProtGPT2 possess functional relevance and improve classification effectiveness". [verified from local PDF text] — same source
- The paper's stated novelty over DeepEfflux is: ProtGPT2-generated sequences, multi-window feature extraction, UMAP + Grad-CAM interpretability, and "improved handling of imbalanced datasets". [verified from local PDF text] — same source
- Per the research brief, the paper's own stated limitations are: **no experimental or structural validation of generated sequences**, and **severe residual imbalance (ABC 2394 vs SMR 18, MATE 102)**. (Provided as task context; consistent with the PDF's framing of data scarcity in low-homology families.)

**Generative-model-as-classifier evidence that favours generative approaches on rare labels:**

- A 2025/2026 study of generative LLMs for protein function prediction reports that its generative model (OPUS-PLLM) "outperforms its sequence-based discriminative baseline, which also uses ESM2-derived features, in both mean MCC and low-frequency-term MCC" — i.e. the advantage is specifically on **long-tail / rare functional labels**, with input representation held constant. Exact per-term numbers are deferred to a supplementary table. [fetched] — [PMC13366677](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366677/)

**Conditional generative augmentation with a non-PLM generative model, evaluated carefully:**

- A 2025 bioRxiv paper builds a two-stage pipeline: few-shot functional-specificity annotation inside a protein family from ~100 labelled sequences, then a **label-aware Restricted Boltzmann Machine** ("a modification of the RBM that integrates the label information together with the aligned sequence") that generates artificial homologs conditioned on the desired label by clamping the label variable during Monte Carlo sampling. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2025.07.22.665933](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.22.665933v1.full)
- In its annotation stage it compares **one-hot MSA encoding, unsupervised RBM embeddings, zero-shot ProtBERT embeddings, and contrastively fine-tuned ProtBERT (few-shot)**; ProtBERT foundation embeddings performed best, and MSA-based methods degraded sharply ("strongly reduced AUC scores") on distant sequences. Generated-sequence consistency reached ≈**0.89 AUC**. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2025.07.22.665933](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.22.665933v1.full)

**Retrieval augmentation as a competing, cheaper form of "augmentation":**

- Retrieved Sequence Augmentation (RSA) augments protein representation learning with retrieved homologs without alignment and reportedly **surpasses MSA Transformer by ~5% on average** across structural and property-prediction tasks. [search snippet, not fetched] — [ACL 2024 EMNLP main 104](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.104.pdf); preprint [bioRxiv 2023.02.22.529597](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.02.22.529597.full.pdf)
- Nucleotide augmentation (back-translating protein sequences into synonymous DNA codon variants) was proposed as a label-preserving augmentation for ML-guided protein engineering. [search snippet, not fetched] — [Bioinformatics Advances vbac094](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformaticsadvances/article/3/1/vbac094/6885439)

**Tabular / cross-domain controlled comparisons of generative oversampling vs SMOTE:**

- A comparative study on **imbalanced protein subcellular localization (tabular features)** compared baseline, SMOTE, CTGAN, TVAE and TabDDPM; search snippets report that **SMOTE and TabDDPM gave the highest F1** and that TabDDPM reached **F1 0.71** vs **0.60 (no augmentation)** and **0.66 (SMOTE)** with random forest. I could not retrieve the full text (MDPI returned HTTP 403), so treat the numbers as snippet-level. [snippet only, fetch blocked] — [Applied Sciences 16(8):3694, doi:10.3390/app16083694](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16083694)
- SMOTE's known failure mode is stated in that literature as: it interpolates between a minority point and its k nearest neighbours, "does not model the underlying joint feature distribution and may introduce noisy synthetic instances in regions near overlapping class boundaries". [snippet only] — [doi:10.3390/app16083694](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16083694)
- Focal loss and class-balanced focal loss are the standard strong baselines in this space: focal loss down-weights well-classified examples via a modulating factor while up-weighting the minority class, and frozen-ESM-2 + MLP pipelines trained with class-balanced focal loss are already in use for imbalanced protein classification. [search-level summary, not fetched] — surfaced via search; see e.g. [Hierarchical clustering-based coarse-to-fine microbial protein function prediction, PMC12750710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12750710)

### Inferences

- The mCNN-GenEfflux improvements (MCC 0.44 → 0.93 in one class) are **implausibly large for a pure data-augmentation effect** on a fixed, homology-split test set. The two benign explanations are (i) the minority classes were so tiny that the pre-augmentation model was essentially untrained on them, or (ii) augmentation also changed the effective class prior. The non-benign explanation is leakage: generated sequences derived from a family are near-duplicates of real family members, and if generation happened before (or across) the train/test split, or if the test set was not clustered away from the generation seed set, the test becomes partly a memorisation test. **The paper reports a 30% identity filter on the fine-tuning corpus, not an identity audit of generated-vs-test sequences** — so this cannot be ruled out from the paper alone. This is the most defensible thesis angle.
- Because Shen et al. found that even *shuffled* (information-destroying) sequences improved downstream metrics, a gain from ProtGPT2 sequences is **not by itself evidence of biological realism** — a regularisation/noise-injection explanation is equally consistent. Any thesis claiming "generated sequences are functionally relevant" needs a control arm with deliberately unrealistic synthetic sequences (shuffled, Markov-sampled at matched amino-acid composition).
- The strongest available cross-domain prior is that deep generative oversampling beats SMOTE only by a **few F1 points**, not by 0.5 MCC. So the expected honest result of a controlled study is "generative augmentation ≈ class weighting / focal loss, sometimes slightly better" — which is a perfectly publishable negative-leaning result and a realistic undergraduate outcome.

### Gaps

- **No study found** that holds the classifier fixed and compares, on the same protein-family benchmark: (a) no augmentation, (b) class weighting, (c) focal loss, (d) random oversampling, (e) SMOTE/ADASYN in PLM embedding space, (f) mixup/manifold-mixup on embeddings, (g) PLM-generated sequence augmentation. I searched for this specifically and found only partial, non-protein, or non-controlled instances.
- No paper found that reports **identity distribution between generated sequences and the held-out test set** for an augmentation experiment on protein families.
- Could not retrieve full text of the MDPI protein-localization augmentation comparison (403) or of the multilabel-oversampling subcellular-localization paper (PMC9825308 served a reCAPTCHA page), so their exact protocols are unverified.

## Q2. How do papers check generated-sequence quality and leakage (identity to test set, pLDDT, novelty/diversity)?

### Takeaway

The generative-protein-design community has a fairly standardised quality/novelty/diversity battery (ESMFold pLDDT/pTM, self-consistency RMSD, Foldseek/TM-score novelty, clustering density, Fréchet distance in sequence space), and the protein-ML benchmarking community has a standardised anti-leakage protocol (MMseqs2/CD-HIT cluster-level splits at ~30% identity). **Application papers that use generation for augmentation generally import neither battery** — they report downstream accuracy instead, which is exactly where the gap is.

### Cited Findings

**Benchmarks and metric definitions:**

- ProteinBench is "a holistic evaluation framework designed to enhance the transparency of protein foundation models", evaluating across **four dimensions: quality, novelty, diversity, and robustness**, over tasks from 3D structure prediction and protein design to conformational dynamics; dataset, code and a public leaderboard were released. (Abstract verified; I could not extract the per-metric table — the PDF fetch returned binary.) [abstract fetched] — [arXiv 2409.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06744)
- Commonly reported metrics in the generative-protein literature, as summarised across the search results: **average pLDDT and pTM from ESMFold** (local-geometry and global-topology confidence), **designability / self-consistency** (generated structure within ≤2 Å scRMSD of the ESMFold prediction of its own designed sequence, with mean pLDDT ≥ 70), **diversity** as mean pairwise sequence identity or mean pairwise TM-score among designable samples (lower = more diverse), **novelty** as the TM-score to the most similar natural protein found by **Foldseek** (lower = more novel), **CD₀.₅** clustering density at 50% sequence identity, and **FD-seq** (Fréchet distance in sequence-embedding space) for distributional match to natural proteins. pLDDT ≥ 80 is the usual "high confidence" threshold. [search-level synthesis across multiple arXiv papers; individual numbers not fetched] — [ProteinBench arXiv 2409.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06744), [La-Proteina arXiv 2507.09466](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09466), [Genie 2 arXiv 2405.15489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15489), [latent-graph-diffusion sequence generation arXiv 2407.07443](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.07443), [DiMA / diffusion on LM encodings arXiv 2403.03726](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03726)
- A concrete example of generation-set filtering in practice: ZymCTRL was fine-tuned on an AlphaFold-Atlas subset restricted to **pLDDT > 80, non-fragmented, 70–100 aa**, giving **143,707 representative sequences after clustering at 30% sequence identity**. [search snippet from fetched-adjacent page, not fully fetched] — [Conditional enzyme generation with adapters, arXiv 2410.03634](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03634v1)
- ProtGPT2's original paper is the reference point for "generated sequences look natural" claims (amino-acid propensities, disorder, predicted structures). — [Ferruz, Schmidt & Höcker, Nat Commun 13:4348 (2022)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-32007-7)
- The label-aware RBM paper's quality battery is a good template for a *classification*-oriented thesis: **PCA projection of natural vs synthetic sequences, Pearson correlation of label-specific connected correlation matrices, and self-consistency (re-label the generated sequence and check agreement with the conditioning label, ROC/AUC ≈ 0.89)**. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2025.07.22.665933](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.22.665933v1.full)

**Leakage protocols:**

- Cluster-level (not random) splitting with **MMseqs2** is now the default anti-leakage protocol: typical settings reported are clustering at **30% sequence identity with coverage 0.8, cov-mode 0, cluster-mode 2**, assigning each cluster to exactly one of train/val/test; some benchmarks use 20% identity / 20% coverage. Thresholds across benchmarks range ~30–90% identity. [search-level synthesis] — [SpanSeq, arXiv 2402.14482](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14482); `[unverified snippet]` leads: [AMPBench-MT arXiv 2607.25518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25518), [SafeBench-Seq arXiv 2512.17527](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17527), [PepBenchmark arXiv 2604.10531](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10531)
- SpanSeq is a dedicated similarity-based sequence-data splitting method motivated by "improved development and assessment of deep learning projects" in biology — i.e. the explicit argument that random splits overestimate performance. [search snippet] — [arXiv 2402.14482](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14482)
- The augmentation-specific leakage mechanism is documented (outside protein work): applying augmentation **before** the train/test split lets synthetic samples derived from training instances appear in the evaluation set, violating independence and causing "substantial overestimation of model performance"; in one migraine-classification case, SMOTE applied globally before partitioning produced artificially inflated accuracy, and performance "drops substantially" once augmentation is restricted to training folds inside stratified CV. `[unverified snippet — arXiv ID is future-dated and I did not fetch it]` — [arXiv 2605.23453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23453)
- Synthetic oversampling also leaks *privacy*, not just performance: a 2025 study of SMOTE reports membership-inference AUC ≈ **0.68**, with minority records most exposed and a **~17% AUC increase** for augmented vs real-data training. [search snippet] — [SMOTE and Mirrors, arXiv 2510.15083](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15083)
- NeoGen-BC's filtering approach named in the brief — keeping generated peptides inside the **convex hull of amino-acid composition statistics** of the real set — is a composition-space plausibility filter rather than an identity/leakage control. (Provided as task context; I did not independently locate the paper in this session.)

### Inferences

- There is a clean methodological asymmetry to exploit: the *design* community measures novelty (how far from natural proteins) because novelty is the goal; the *augmentation* community wants the opposite (synthetic sequences inside the class manifold) but has no agreed metric for "close enough to be informative, far enough not to be a duplicate". A thesis that defines and measures this trade-off curve — e.g. sweep generation temperature / top-k, measure max-identity-to-train and max-identity-to-test with MMseqs2 or BLAST, and plot downstream minority-class MCC against those identities — would be a genuinely new contribution and is computationally cheap.
- The correct protocol for an augmentation experiment, synthesising the above: cluster the real data with MMseqs2 at 30% identity → split by cluster → fine-tune the generator **only on the training clusters** → generate → run a **leakage audit** of generated vs test (and vs validation) with MMseqs2 search or BLAST, discarding or at least reporting any generated sequence above an identity threshold → only then train the classifier. I found no protein-family augmentation paper that documents all of these steps, which makes "augmentation under a leakage-controlled protocol" a defensible thesis framing rather than a reproduction.
- ESMFold pLDDT on a Colab/Kaggle budget is feasible for a few thousand short-to-medium sequences but not for tens of thousands of long transporter sequences (efflux proteins are frequently 400–1000+ aa, and ESMFold memory scales badly). A thesis should plan pLDDT on a **stratified sample**, not the whole generated set.

### Gaps

- I could not extract ProteinBench's per-metric definitions and model-by-model results (the PDF fetch returned binary content and the abstract page lacks the tables). The metric definitions above are a cross-paper synthesis and should be re-verified against the ProteinBench HTML or leaderboard before being quoted with thresholds.
- No source found that proposes a *standard* identity threshold for declaring a generated sequence "leaked" relative to a test set. This appears to be genuinely unsettled — which is itself a finding.
- I did not verify the NeoGen-BC (2026) convex-hull filtering claim against the primary source.

## Q3. Few-shot / metric-learning / prototypical approaches for protein family classification with PLM embeddings — and what is untested on transporter/efflux families?

### Takeaway

Contrastive/metric learning on PLM embeddings is well established for enzyme EC numbers (CLEAN) and increasingly for protein family and substrate prediction, and prototypical/Siamese few-shot networks on ProtBERT exist for protein families. For **transporters specifically**, the literature is dominated by fine-tuned-BERT classifiers (TooT-* family, PortPred, DeepEfflux, GenEfflux); I found **no CLEAN-style contrastive/prototypical metric-learning model for efflux or transporter superfamilies**, which is the most concrete untested combination.

### Cited Findings

**Contrastive learning for enzyme function (the template to transfer):**

- CLEAN frames EC-number prediction as multi-label classification in a learned embedding space where **Euclidean distance reflects functional similarity** — same EC → small distance, different EC → large distance — and reports better accuracy, reliability and sensitivity than BLASTp; it can annotate understudied enzymes, correct mislabelled ones, and identify promiscuous enzymes with ≥2 EC numbers, with experimental validation on enzymes including MJ1651 and SsFIA. [search-level synthesis of the Science 2023 paper] — [paper PDF (Zhao group)](https://zhaogroup.chbe.illinois.edu/publications/HZ406.pdf); [code](https://github.com/tttianhao/CLEAN); [publication record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/enzyme-function-prediction-using-contrastive-learning/)
- CLEAN-Contact extends this by combining amino-acid sequence with **protein structure (contact-map) representations** for improved enzyme functional annotation. — [Commun Biol s42003-024-07359-z](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-024-07359-z); [preprint](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-4207875/v1_covered_8dc03cd8-e3b3-4748-866b-8eb33c6affc1.pdf)
- A transformer-layer deep-learning approach for functional annotation of enzyme-encoding genes is the other main comparison point in this subfield. — [Nat Commun s41467-023-43216-z](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-43216-z)

**Few-shot architectures for protein families:**

- "An Explainable Deep Few-shot Network for Protein Family Classification" uses a **deep Siamese network with two identical ProtBert encoders** plus transfer learning, producing embeddings of both sequences in a pair. — [CCAI / caiac proceedings](https://caiac.pubpub.org/pub/7xk5hhz5); [PDF record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371366158_An_Explainable_Deep_Few-shot_Network_for_Protein_Family_Classification)
- Prototypical Networks are the canonical few-shot baseline: learn an embedding where each class is represented by the mean (prototype) of its support examples and classify by distance to prototypes. — [Snell et al., NeurIPS 2017](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~zemel/documents/prototypical_networks_nips_2017.pdf)
- The label-aware-RBM paper (above) shows **contrastively fine-tuned ProtBERT in a genuine few-shot regime (~100 labelled sequences)** for fine-grained functional specificity inside families, with zero-shot ProtBERT embeddings already strong. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2025.07.22.665933](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.22.665933v1.full)
- Contrastive learning on PLM representations is also used for residue-level tasks, e.g. protein–DNA binding-site prediction from a pre-trained PLM plus contrastive learning — relevant precedent for the "residue classes" half of the brief. — [arXiv 2306.15912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.15912)

**What already exists for transporters (the competitive landscape):**

- TooT-BERT-T discriminates transport from non-transport proteins using BERT representations, reported at **accuracy 93.89%, MCC 0.86**. — [Springer chapter, doi:10.1007/978-3-031-17024-9_1](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-17024-9_1)
- Sibling models: TooT-BERT-M (membrane proteins, **92.46% / MCC 0.85**) and TooT-BERT-C (ion channels vs membrane proteins). — same chapter / search-level summary
- Substrate specificity: three ProtBERT-based approaches classify sequences into **12 specific substrates**, with fine-tuned BERT reaching **MCC 0.95 / accuracy 99.3%**. — [bioRxiv 2022.07.23.501263](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.07.23.501263v1.full)
- TooT-SS uses ProtBERT-BFD transfer learning to predict **96 specific substrates** with **F1 0.92 / MCC 0.91**. — [BIO Web of Conferences (ICBBB 2025)](https://www.bio-conferences.org/articles/bioconf/abs/2025/14/bioconf_icbbb2025_01001/bioconf_icbbb2025_01001.html)
- TooTranslator (Nov 2025) does **zero-shot substrate classification for transport proteins by aligning protein and chemical language-model embeddings**, explicitly motivated by "few-shot learning from few training samples in each class and potentially zero-shot learning ... by reasoning through semantic similarity". This is the closest existing work to a metric-learning approach on transporters. — [bioRxiv 2025.11.13.688294](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.13.688294.full.pdf)
- PortPred identifies transporters and their substrates from deep-learning embeddings of amino-acid sequences. — [J Cell Biochem, doi:10.1002/jcb.30490](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jcb.30490)
- TCDB is the reference transporter classification database and is what GenEfflux/DeepEfflux draw their labels from. [verified from the advisors' PDF text] — /home/ngthtrong/CT553H/docs/common/advisor/Prof Ho/1-s2.0-S1476927125002567-main.pdf
- A 2025 review of transformer-based language models for protein sequence analysis and design is a useful single citation for the landscape. — [arXiv 2507.13646](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.13646)

### Inferences

- The transporter/efflux subfield has converged on "fine-tune a BERT-family PLM, report accuracy/MCC". Nobody in that subfield appears to have applied the **CLEAN recipe** (supervised contrastive / triplet loss on frozen PLM embeddings, classify by distance to class centroids, with max-separation or SupCon sampling). Because CLEAN's selling point is precisely that it handles **understudied classes with few examples better than BLAST**, and because efflux superfamilies are exactly that (SMR n=18), this transfer is both the most natural and the most defensible novelty claim available to the student.
- A prototypical/centroid classifier has a structural advantage over softmax on extreme imbalance: the class prototype for SMR is computed from its 18 members regardless of how many ABC sequences exist, so the decision rule is not dominated by the majority prior. This predicts that **metric learning may make generative augmentation unnecessary** — which is the sharpest possible thesis hypothesis given the advisors' prior work, and it directly engages their stated limitation rather than merely extending their method.
- With n=18 for SMR, any test set for that class has single-digit size, so point estimates of MCC/F1 will be extremely noisy. A credible thesis must use **repeated stratified cross-validation at the cluster level with confidence intervals**, not a single split — otherwise the result is uninterpretable regardless of method. (Cluster-aware confidence intervals are already an advertised feature of recent homology-controlled benchmarks: `[unverified snippet]` [SafeBench-Seq arXiv 2512.17527](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17527).)

### Gaps

- I found no paper applying prototypical networks or supervised contrastive learning to **efflux superfamily** (ABC/MFS/MATE/RND/SMR) classification; absence of evidence here is from targeted searching, but I cannot prove absence.
- I did not find a public, standard, homology-split benchmark for efflux superfamily classification. DeepEfflux and GenEfflux each built their own TCDB-derived sets; whether the GenEfflux dataset is downloadable was not verified in this session.
- The "residue classes" half of the brief (e.g. rare residue-level annotations such as specific binding or modification sites) is covered here only thinly — I found contrastive-learning precedent for protein–DNA binding residues but did not survey residue-level imbalance methods systematically.

## Q4. Concrete thesis-scale research questions with measurable outcomes and baselines — and what would be genuinely new?

### Takeaway

The genuinely new contribution available at undergraduate scale is **not a better classifier but a leakage-controlled audit**: does PLM-generative augmentation still help efflux/transporter family classification once (i) the generator sees only training clusters, (ii) generated-to-test identity is measured and reported, and (iii) it is compared against class weighting, focal loss, embedding-space oversampling, and a metric-learning classifier? Every component is cheap, the baselines are well defined, and the result is publishable whether positive or negative.

### Cited Findings (what makes each RQ grounded rather than speculative)

- The advisors' paper **states** the two limitations the RQs attack (no validation of generated sequences; residual severe imbalance), so the work is continuous with their agenda rather than a critique from outside. [task context + PDF framing] — /home/ngthtrong/CT553H/docs/common/advisor/Prof Ho/1-s2.0-S1476927125002567-main.pdf
- Shen et al.'s finding that shuffled sequences can also improve metrics supplies a **ready-made negative control arm** for RQ1. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2021.02.18.431877](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.02.18.431877v1.full)
- CLEAN supplies a **validated, published recipe** (contrastive embedding + distance-to-centroid, strong on understudied classes) to port, so RQ2 is an application of a known-good method to a new domain, not a new architecture. — [Zhao group PDF](https://zhaogroup.chbe.illinois.edu/publications/HZ406.pdf); [code](https://github.com/tttianhao/CLEAN)
- MMseqs2 cluster-level splitting at 30% identity / 0.8 coverage is the accepted protocol, so the experimental design can cite precedent rather than invent one. — [SpanSeq arXiv 2402.14482](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14482)
- ProteinBench's four dimensions (quality, novelty, diversity, robustness) give a citable framework for the generated-sequence audit. — [arXiv 2409.06744](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.06744)
- The ~0.89-AUC "self-consistency relabeling" check is a concrete, cheap generated-sequence quality metric that needs no structure prediction. [fetched] — [bioRxiv 2025.07.22.665933](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.22.665933v1.full)

### Inferences — four candidate thesis RQs, ordered by novelty-per-unit-risk

**RQ-A (recommended core): Does generative augmentation survive a leakage-controlled protocol?**
Design: TCDB efflux superfamilies (or a broader transporter family set). MMseqs2 cluster at 30% identity, split by cluster into 5 folds. For each fold, fine-tune ProtGPT2 **only on that fold's training clusters**; generate N sequences per minority family at several temperatures/top-k; run MMseqs2/BLAST of generated vs test and vs train, reporting the full max-identity distribution; train one fixed classifier (frozen ESM-2 embeddings + MLP, so compute is tiny) on each augmentation condition. Arms: none / random oversample / class weights / focal loss / SMOTE-in-embedding-space / manifold mixup / ProtGPT2-generated / **shuffled-sequence control** / **composition-matched Markov control**. Outcomes: per-class MCC and macro-F1 with CIs over folds; plus the key new plot — **minority-class MCC as a function of generated-to-train and generated-to-test identity**. Genuinely new: the identity-vs-benefit curve and the negative controls; nobody in this subfield has published either.

**RQ-B (strong companion): Does metric learning remove the need for augmentation?**
Port CLEAN's supervised-contrastive + distance-to-centroid recipe to efflux/transporter superfamilies on frozen ESM-2 embeddings; compare against softmax MLP with class weighting/focal loss, against GenEfflux's reported numbers, and against BLASTp/PSI-BLAST nearest-neighbour as a classical baseline. Outcome: per-class MCC on the 18-sequence SMR class, and a 1-shot/5-shot curve (train with k sequences per family, k ∈ {1,3,5,10,all}). Genuinely new: first contrastive/prototypical model for efflux superfamilies; plus the interaction term — does augmentation still add anything **on top of** metric learning?

**RQ-C (cheap add-on, high reviewer value): Which generator is worth the compute?**
Hold the classifier and protocol fixed and swap the generator: fine-tuned ProtGPT2 vs fine-tuned ProGen2-small vs EvoDiff conditional/inpainting vs ESM-2 masked-resampling of real family members vs no generator. Report cost (GPU-hours) alongside benefit. Feasible on 45 GPU-hours/week only if the classifier is a frozen-embedding MLP and generation counts are modest. Genuinely new: no such like-for-like generator comparison for augmentation of an imbalanced family-classification task was found.

**RQ-D (scope-reduction fallback): Audit-only thesis.**
If compute or time runs short, drop the classifier-improvement claim and deliver a pure methodological audit: reproduce GenEfflux-style augmentation, quantify how much of the reported gain is attributable to random-vs-cluster splitting and to generated-test similarity, and publish the protocol + benchmark split as a reusable artifact. Lower ceiling, very low risk, still a real contribution.

**Scoping advice (inference):** the biology knowledge required for RQ-A/B is modest — labels come from TCDB, no wet-lab interpretation is needed, and the hard parts are MMseqs2 bookkeeping and honest statistics, which are software skills. RQ-C is the one that can silently eat the GPU budget. Prefer **frozen PLM embeddings + small heads** over fine-tuning PLMs end-to-end; that single choice is what makes all of this fit in Colab Pro + Kaggle.
